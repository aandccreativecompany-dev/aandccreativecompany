Are You Programmed to Stay Stuck? How Neuroplasticity Helps You Break Old Patterns

By A&C Creative Ventures · 4 November 2026 · 3 min read

I have been thinking about why we repeat the same patterns, even when we badly want something to change. We make fresh plans. We set new goals. We work at them for a while. And somehow, a few months later, we are standing in almost the same place.

For about ten years I have been reading, repeatedly, about how the mind works, and one explanation keeps proving useful. It is called neuroplasticity, and I would like to break it down in plain language, because it is not just a buzzword.

What neuroplasticity means

Neuroplasticity is the brain's ability to change its own structure and connections in response to experience. Every time you think a thought, feel an emotion, or repeat an action, particular networks of brain cells fire together. The more often they fire together, the easier it becomes for them to do so again. A well-known short-hand for this is "cells that fire together, wire together."

It works like a path through a field. Walk it once and there is barely a trace. Walk it a thousand times and you have a clear track that your feet follow without any thought.

Living on autopilot

When we keep thinking the same thoughts, feeling the same emotions and behaving in the same ways, those patterns can become deeply ingrained. We call this being hard-wired, though it is more like being well-worn. Our reactions arrive before we have chosen them. We end up living on autopilot.

This is not a personal failing. The brain is efficient, and it likes to automate what you do often. That is why you can drive a familiar route while thinking about something else. The same efficiency also means that unhelpful patterns, such as snapping under stress, assuming the worst, or giving up when it gets hard, become automatic in exactly the same way.

Three steps to un-programme a pattern

  1. Become aware of the automatic thoughts and reactions. You cannot change what you cannot see. For a week, simply notice. When you feel the familiar spike of anxiety, irritation or avoidance, pause and name it: "There it is again." Writing it down helps. Awareness alone often loosens the grip.
  2. Practise responding differently. Once you spot the pattern, choose a small alternative. If you usually go quiet when challenged, try asking one question. If you usually reach for your phone when you feel stuck, try three slow breaths first. It will feel awkward. That awkwardness is what a new path feels like.
  3. Give your brain new experiences to learn from. Change is not only about stopping the old behaviour. The brain also needs fresh input: new people, new places, new skills, new ways of doing familiar things. These give it something different to build on.

That, in simple terms, is neuroplasticity at work.

Wishing is not the same as becoming

Here is the part I find most useful. Change does not happen because you wished for it. It happens as you become it, one repeated choice at a time.

You cannot build a different future by repeatedly practising your past. If every day looks like the one before, the pattern will simply deepen. But a small, deliberate difference, repeated often enough, begins to lay a new path.

A note on realism

It is worth being honest about the limits. Rewiring is gradual, and it does not happen on demand. Some patterns, especially those linked to trauma, anxiety, depression or other health concerns, benefit from the support of a qualified professional, and there is no shame in seeking it. Neuroplasticity is a hopeful idea, not a promise that willpower alone can fix everything.

What it does tell us is that we are not fixed. Even deeply ingrained habits can shift when we bring awareness, practice and new experience to them.

Try this today

Pick one automatic reaction you would like to change. For the next seven days, write down each time it happens and what triggered it. On day eight, choose one small alternative response and practise it for another week.
