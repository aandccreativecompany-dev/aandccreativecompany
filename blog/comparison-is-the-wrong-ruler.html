Comparison Is the Wrong Ruler

Why your timeline was never supposed to match anyone else's — and how to actually let that go.

A&C Creative Ventures · Motivation

Social comparison isn't a modern glitch — it's a documented, decades-old feature of how people evaluate themselves. Psychologist Leon Festinger's 1954 social comparison theory proposed that, absent an objective standard, people judge their own abilities and progress by measuring against others. The theory is old. The scale at which we now do it isn't.

What happens when the comparison never stops

A 2018 study at the University of Pennsylvania, led by psychologist Melissa Hunt, tested this directly: researchers assigned some participants to limit their social media use to about 10 minutes per platform per day, while a control group used it as usual. After three weeks, the group with limited use showed significant reductions in loneliness and depression compared to the control group — with the sharpest drops among participants who started with higher depression symptoms.

In Hunt et al.'s 2018 study, just three weeks of limiting social media to about 10 minutes per platform per day produced measurable drops in loneliness and depression versus unrestricted use.

The researchers' interpretation lines up with what the comparison itself is doing: social feeds are a highlight reel by design, and constant exposure to other people's curated peaks — while you're living your own ordinary, unfiltered middle — produces exactly the kind of self-comparison Festinger described, at a volume no generation before this one ever had to process.

Your timeline was never a shared one

The problem isn't that comparison happens — that's built in. The problem is treating someone else's highlight reel as your ruler for what "on track" is supposed to look like. Their timeline reflects a different starting point, different resources, and a version of events you're only seeing the edited parts of.

  1. Audit what you're comparing against. A curated feed is not a peer's actual, average day — treat it accordingly.
  2. Build a real limit, not a vague intention. The research used a specific cap — about 10 minutes per platform — not "I'll cut back."
  3. Compare yourself to your own last month, not someone else's highlight reel — it's the only comparison with a fair baseline.
Do This Today

Set a genuine time limit on one social app for the next three weeks — 10 minutes a day. Notice not just your mood, but how often "I'm behind" thoughts show up once the comparison volume drops.

Sources
