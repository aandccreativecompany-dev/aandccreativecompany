Be the One Who Moves: You Cannot Get Proof Before You Start

By A&C Creative Ventures · 12 November 2026 · 4 min read

The need for proof has become exhausting. And it is exactly what keeps so many of us from moving forward.

We want evidence that something will work before we begin it. We want confidence before we speak. We want certainty before the results come in, and proof that people will like our work before we dare to make it public.

Asking for the finish-line evidence at the starting line

Stop for a second and look at that request. How can you have evidence for something you have not done yet? You are standing at the starting line asking for the proof that only exists after the finish line.

It is a reasonable-sounding instinct. Nobody wants to waste effort. But it creates a loop with no exit: you cannot act until you have proof, and you cannot get proof until you act.

Some evidence only comes after action

The way out is to recognise that there are two kinds of evidence. Some you can gather beforehand: you can research, compare, ask people who have done it, and look at how others fared. That is worth doing, and worth doing properly.

But a different kind of evidence only appears after you start. Whether your idea resonates. Whether you enjoy the work. Whether the conversation leads somewhere. Whether you are better at it than you feared. You cannot think your way to that information. You have to go and collect it.

So we apply. We learn. We experiment. We start conversations. And then, only then, the proof arrives in the form of outcomes.

Make the first move smaller

If the start feels too big, make it smaller until it feels almost silly. The aim is not to bet everything. It is to generate real information.

  • Instead of "launch the business", sell one thing to one person.
  • Instead of "write the book", write one page and send it to a friend.
  • Instead of "change careers", have two conversations with people already doing the work.
  • Instead of "become a confident speaker", say one thing in the next meeting.
  • Instead of "post a perfect video", publish an imperfect one and read the comments.

Each of these small moves produces something that thinking alone cannot: a result you can learn from.

What to do with the results

Treat each outcome as data, not a ruling. If something works, do more of it. If it does not, ask what the result is telling you: perhaps the idea needs adjusting, perhaps the audience is different from what you assumed, perhaps it just needs more time. Then take the next small step, with better information than before.

Over time, this is how confidence is actually built. Not before action, but from it. Confidence is the evidence of having done things, accumulating.

Participate

You have to participate in life for it to show you proof. That is the uncomfortable part, and also the freeing one, because it means you do not have to be certain first. You only have to be willing.

Be the one who moves.

Why we ask for proof

Wanting proof is not foolish. It is a way of protecting ourselves from embarrassment, wasted time and rejection. Having been judged or disappointed before, we try to make sure it does not happen again by getting the answer in advance.

But the protection costs more than it gives. When every move has to be preceded by certainty, you stay in the same place while others who were willing to try, imperfectly, collect experience. Experience compounds in a way that waiting never does.

Signs you are waiting for proof you cannot get

  • You keep researching long after you have enough information to begin.
  • You wait for a feeling of readiness that never quite arrives.
  • You ask others for reassurance repeatedly, but do not act on it.
  • You start new courses, notebooks or plans in place of the thing itself.
  • You imagine that other people started with confidence, when most of them started with questions.

If you recognise yourself here, be kind about it. The pattern is common and it is very fixable. The fix is not courage in the dramatic sense. It is a small, ordinary first step, repeated.

Try this today

Choose one thing you have been waiting to feel sure about. Write down the smallest action that would give you real information, something that could be done within 48 hours, and do it. Then write down what you learned.
