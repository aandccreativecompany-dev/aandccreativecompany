The Cheat Code: Stop Preparing for Failure Before You Have Tried

By A&C Creative Ventures · 12 November 2026 · 4 min read

How you enter a situation matters more than most of us admit. Sometimes it matters more than the situation itself.

If you walk into something already expecting it to fail, it very often will. Not through magic, but because before you have even tried, you have already imagined everything that could go wrong. You have rehearsed the disaster in detail.

Fear in a sensible disguise

Most of us call this being realistic. "I am just being practical." "I do not want to get my hopes up." But a lot of what passes for realism is really fear wearing logic as a costume.

Overthinking, hesitation, delay and the endless search for reasons not to start all arrive looking reasonable. Each one sounds like caution. Together they add up to a decision not to try, made so gradually that it never feels like a decision.

And eventually you receive the outcome you expected. You were, after all, well prepared for it. You had prepped for failure.

This is not blind optimism

I want to be clear about what I am not saying. I am not suggesting that you pretend risks do not exist or that positive thinking guarantees success. It does not, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

What I am describing is a willingness to give yourself a fair chance. To approach things with the working assumption that they could actually work.

Prepare for risks, of course. Check the numbers, build a safety net, have a plan B. But do not make that your only preparation. Risks do not decide the outcome. You do.

What changes when you expect it could work

  • You prepare differently. Instead of only asking "What could go wrong?", you also ask "What would I need for this to go well?" The second question produces a different list of actions.
  • You act sooner. Expectation of failure makes waiting feel safe. Expectation of a fair chance makes starting feel worthwhile.
  • You notice more opportunities. People who expect things to work tend to see openings that fear-focused people scroll straight past.
  • You recover faster. If it does not work, you are more likely to treat it as information, and less likely to treat it as proof of what you suspected all along.

Give success more than one chance

One thought I come back to is this: stop behaving as if failure is confirmed. Give success the chance to surprise you. And notice that I said chances, plural.

Not every attempt works. But a first try that falls short is not a verdict. It is one chance used, with others still available, and with more knowledge in your hands than before.

A simple two-column exercise

Next time you are hesitating, take a sheet of paper and draw two columns.

  1. In the first column, list the risks. Be honest and specific, and next to each one, write what you would do to reduce it or respond to it.
  2. In the second column, list what would need to be true for this to work, and what you can do this week to make each item more likely.
  3. Compare the columns. If the second is empty, you have not yet given success a fair hearing.

Why the mind rehearses failure

Anticipating what could go wrong is a protective habit. It kept our ancestors alive, and in some situations it still serves us well. The trouble is that the mind treats imagined danger almost like real danger: your body responds to the rehearsal with tension, hesitation and avoidance. You can end up exhausted by a failure that never actually happened.

That is not a reason to switch off your caution. It is a reason to put it to work in a particular place: planning, not deciding. Let the worried part of you build the safety net. Then let the rest of you walk across.

A small experiment

Try this for a week. Before any task you feel unsure about, whether it is an email, a conversation or a first draft, take one minute and say what you expect to happen. Afterwards, write down what actually happened. Most people find that reality is kinder than the prediction far more often than they expected. That gap between forecast and result is some of the most useful evidence you will ever collect about your own mind.

Try this today

Pick one thing you have been avoiding because you expect it to go badly. Write down the best realistic outcome, not the fantasy but the genuinely plausible one, and one action that makes it more likely. Do that action today.
