What Are You Willing to Settle For? How Standards Quietly Slip

By A&C Creative Ventures · 12 November 2026 · 4 min read

Think about how you approach your dreams. You usually begin with big ambitions, clear goals and a lot of excitement. The first few weeks feel electric. And then, gradually, something shifts. You start lowering your expectations.

It rarely happens in one dramatic moment. It happens in small, reasonable-sounding adjustments, and that is exactly why it is easy to miss.

How the bar slips

I have noticed this in myself. After a stretch of not seeing results, my expectations dropped a little. My work dropped a little too. Nothing collapsed. I simply began to ask for less.

Here is the mechanism, as I understand it. When we do not count small steps as progress, we decide that things are hard. Because things are hard, we tell ourselves we were asking for too much. The goal gets trimmed, then trimmed again, until it fits comfortably inside our current circumstances.

We end up settling for less, not because we lack the ability, but because our standards quietly dropped. The ability was never the thing that changed.

Raise the bar, but do not confuse that with perfection

The answer is not to demand flawless work from yourself. Perfectionism is its own trap: it makes starting feel dangerous and finishing feel impossible. What I mean by a higher standard is something simpler.

  • Keep showing up. A modest effort that continues is worth far more than a heroic effort that stops.
  • Refuse to abandon something meaningful just because progress is slow. Slow is not the same as failing.
  • Define progress by inputs, not only outcomes. You cannot fully control results, but you can count the days you did the work.
  • Keep the dream the right size. Do not shrink it to fit where you are today. Let it stay as big as it honestly is.

A tip for overthinkers

If you are like me, someone who thinks hard about everything, you may find that planning becomes a place to hide. The beautiful framework never quite gets finished, so the work never quite begins.

What helps me is to reverse the order: get it out there first, then build the structure around it. Publish the rough draft. Make the first call. Run the first version of the experiment. A messy start gives you something real to organise. A perfect plan gives you nothing to improve.

Grow into the person who can do it

When circumstances feel too small for a dream, there are two ways to respond. One is to shrink the dream. The other is to grow into the person who can make it happen, slowly, through repeated practice and small wins.

The second path is slower at the start, but it is the only one that gets you anywhere you actually wanted to go.

A quick check on your own standards

  1. What did I hope for when I started this?
  2. What am I now telling myself is "good enough"?
  3. Is that conclusion based on evidence, or on tiredness and slow progress?
  4. What is one small action I can take this week that matches my original standard?

If you answer honestly, you will often find that the dream has not changed. Only your belief in it has.

Why slow progress feels like failure

Progress in anything that matters is lumpy. For long periods, you are doing the work and nothing visible changes. The learning happens below the surface, in skills, habits and understanding that have not yet turned into results. This is the stage where most people quietly lower their sights, just before the visible change begins.

Keeping a simple record helps. A running list of what you did each week, however small, gives you an honest picture of your effort. On discouraging days it is hard to argue with a page showing twelve weeks of steady work.

Signs your standards are slipping

  • You describe your goal using words like "just" or "only" ("I only want to...").
  • You find yourself comparing your plan to the easiest version, not the one you first imagined.
  • You celebrate finishing rather than doing it well, every time.
  • You say "it is fine" more often than you mean it.
  • You feel relief, not disappointment, when a goal is postponed.

Noticing one or two of these is normal. Noticing most of them is a sign to revisit what you actually want, and to ask whether the goal has changed, or whether only your confidence has.

Try this today

Write down one goal you have quietly downgraded. Next to it, write the original version. Then choose one small, concrete action that belongs to the original version and do it in the next 48 hours.
